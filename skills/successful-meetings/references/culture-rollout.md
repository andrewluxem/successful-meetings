# Rolling out a better meeting culture

Fixing one meeting is a repair. Changing how a team meets is a rollout, and rollouts fail when they arrive as mandates. Six steps, in order.

**1. Introduce the concept.** Present the structure in a team meeting: the four tenets, the agenda format, the follow-up format. Use a recent real meeting as the before picture. Keep it short; the demonstration in step 3 does the persuading.

**2. Start small.** Change one thing first, usually the agenda-with-objective shipped 24 hours ahead. Then add on-time starts. Then the follow-up format. Incremental changes stick; a ten-rule regime announced on Monday is dead by Friday.

**3. Role model it.** The leader shows up on time, ships the pre-read, states the objective, ends five minutes early with decisions and owners. Observed behavior sets the standard. Nothing written down survives a leader who ignores it.

**4. Encourage and train.** Rotate meeting ownership so others run the format. Coach privately where needed, especially on facilitation and on asking better questions.

**5. Gather feedback.** Ask what is working and what is not, through quick surveys or open discussion. The format serves the team, not the reverse; adjust it where it grinds.

**6. Recognize and reinforce.** When someone runs a tight meeting or writes a follow-up note that lands, say so visibly. Reinforcement is what converts a practice into a culture.

## Why the follow-up is the hinge

Two identical meetings can produce opposite outcomes based on what happens in the next 24 hours. The meeting with no note fades: enthusiasm decays, ownership blurs, and the ideas become memories. The meeting whose note arrives the next morning, with decisions, owners, and dates, keeps its momentum because everyone knows what they signed up for and when it is due. The difference between a meeting with potential and a meeting with results is almost always the follow-up, which is why this skill refuses to leave action items unowned.
