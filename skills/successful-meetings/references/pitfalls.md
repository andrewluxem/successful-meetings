# Failure modes and counter-strategies

## The markers of a bad meeting

Late starts. No agenda or purpose. Overruns. Too many people. Not the right people. No decisions made. No follow-up. Last-minute invites. Recording without permission. Attention leaking into side channels while someone is presenting.

Any one of these is survivable. Three or more in the same recurring meeting means the meeting needs an audit, not another occurrence.

## Four pitfalls and what beats them

**1. Late starts.** A late start taxes everyone who showed up on time, and the tax compounds into disengagement.
Counter: start exactly on time, regardless of who is missing. Punctuality is set by observed behavior, not by policy. Latecomers catch up from the notes.

**2. No clear agenda.** Without a stated objective the meeting drifts, and the room can feel it drifting.
Counter: circulate a concise agenda with a one-sentence objective at least 24 hours in advance. If the owner cannot write the objective sentence, the meeting is not ready to be scheduled.

**3. Irrelevant attendees.** Every attendee without a role converts the meeting into theater for an audience.
Counter: curate. Each invite traces to a role: owner, decider, or contributor. Everyone else is informed via the follow-up note. Fewer people also means decisions come faster.

**4. No decisions or follow-up.** A meeting that ends without decisions and owners was a conversation, and its output evaporates within days.
Counter: reserve the final five minutes to restate decisions and assign actions with owners and dates. Send the note within 24 hours. Check prior actions at the next session, because the checking is what makes assignment real.

## Principles worth keeping

- **Parkinson's Law**: work expands to fill the time available. Applied to meetings, the fix is to shrink the container. Laura Vanderkam's version: decide how long the meeting will be, then cut that time in half.
- **Round-robin**: go around the room and give each participant a turn. It surfaces perspectives the loudest voice would otherwise flatten.
- **Peter Drucker**: "The most important thing in communication is hearing what isn't said." Silence from a contributor is a signal to pull on, not a convenience to accept.

When citing anything beyond these, attribute it. Numbers without sources do not belong in an artifact this skill produces.
