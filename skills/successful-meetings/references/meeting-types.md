# Meeting types and prep depth

Two categories cover nearly every meeting. Classifying first matters because it sets how much preparation the meeting deserves and what "good" looks like at the end.

## Process-oriented meetings

The backbone of day-to-day operations. Recurring, with a stable structure. Their job is alignment and early detection, not novelty.

Examples: one-on-ones, staff meetings, sprint or progress checks, operating reviews such as 4-blocker reviews.

What good looks like: the structure barely changes week to week, prep is light because the format carries the weight, problems surface early, and prior action items get checked every session. If a process meeting keeps producing "no updates," shrink its cadence or make it async.

## Mission-oriented meetings

Ad hoc. They exist to reach a decision, and they die once the decision is made.

Examples: decision meetings, brainstorming sessions, quarterly or strategic planning reviews, working backwards reviews.

What good looks like: pre-reads shipped at least 24 hours ahead, a named decider, a decision protocol stated up front (who decides, by when, with what input), and the meeting ends with the decision written down. A mission meeting that recurs is a warning sign: either the decision is not getting made, or it has quietly become a process meeting nobody redesigned.

## Prep depth by type

| | Process-oriented | Mission-oriented |
|---|---|---|
| Agenda | Standing template, updated inputs | Built fresh around the decision |
| Pre-reads | Optional, brief | Required, shipped 24 hours ahead |
| Attendees | Stable roster | Curated per decision |
| End state | Actions checked and updated | Decision recorded with owners |

## The waste runs both ways

Too many meetings burns time. Too few creates coordination debt: duplicated work, late discoveries, decisions made by hallway default. The target is not fewer meetings, it is purposeful ones. When in doubt, ask what the meeting produces. If the answer is nothing nameable, it is either waste or a doc.
